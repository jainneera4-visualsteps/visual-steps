# Visual Steps product philosophy

Visual Steps helps a parent or caregiver offer meaningful activities as choices and make each chosen activity easier to understand through clear visual steps. It supports autistic children and adults without treating the learner's day as a checklist.

## Choice outside, structure inside

Activities are choices, not a list of tasks the learner must finish. The learner may choose an available activity in any order, choose one, take a break, or choose none. There is no mandatory next activity. Completing one activity does not imply that every other visible activity must be completed. The parent controls which choices are available and appropriate; the learner retains agency among those choices.

Activity → Steps is the core structure. Once an activity is chosen, its steps may be sequential, concrete, visual, and predictable. Step checkmarks can help the learner follow that particular sequence and resume after a break. Predictability should ease uncertainty, not make a flexible plan feel like a rigid rule.

## Explain change without blame

Parents may mark an activity temporarily unavailable, cancelled, or replaced and give a short, learner-facing reason. A replacement is another available choice, not a required next task. An unchosen activity stays in a parent-only planning record under its original date; it is not silently carried forward or presented to the learner as failure. No penalty or negative token adjustment follows an unchosen choice.

## Communication and recognition

The learner must be able to ask for help and communicate with a parent. A help request is information, not failure. Messages support an ongoing conversation; positive recognition separately records something a parent genuinely noticed. Recognition stands on its own without tokens. Bonus tokens are a distinct parent action, and tokens decrease only when a reward is purchased.

## Rewards remain secondary

Activities should be worth choosing for their meaning, usefulness, interest, or enjoyment—not just their tokens. Reward amounts may be visible inside the chosen activity's details, but should not dominate the choice screen. The learner Rewards view shows only active items currently affordable with their balance. It does not display locked goals, progress bars, "only X more" prompts, or a reason to rush through activities. Parents may manage a larger catalog privately.

## Parent depth, learner clarity

Parents retain scheduling, recurrence, verification, learning, history, and other advanced controls. These should not crowd the learner experience. Parent-facing insights help a caregiver notice patterns and adjust support, not judge a single day. The learner should never see streaks, daily completion quotas, tasks remaining, or pressure to complete every visible activity. AI belongs on the parent side only when it provides a clear benefit and does not replace parent judgment.

## Welcoming and accessible

Themes, characters, color, and gentle animation can make Visual Steps inviting. They must preserve contrast, legibility, predictable control placement, and reduced-motion support. Decoration should not obscure words, steps, choices, or family messages. Age-respectful personalization matters for both children and adults.

## Language and decision test

Prefer "Choose an activity," "I’m finished with this activity," and "You may choose another activity, take a break, or leave." A calm completion acknowledgement recognizes the activity just chosen. Avoid language that implies a queue, obligation, race, or prize to unlock.

Before shipping a learner-facing change, ask: Does it preserve choice and Activity → Steps? Does it make the chosen activity clearer? Can the learner stop or ask for help without feeling behind? Does it explain changes simply? Are rewards and metrics subordinate to participation and understanding? If it makes the learner think "these are tasks I must finish," reconsider it.
